# RCSA challenge guidance (synthetic prototype corpus)

Illustrative guidance written for this prototype. It is not an official methodology or regulation.

## Challenging a not applicable response
A First Line owner may mark a risk as not applicable for a process step. Second Line should accept this only when no contradicting evidence exists. Loss events, an assigned KRI, an earlier RCSA entry or a described control all contradict a not applicable response and require the owner to explain the decision. The absence of loss events alone does not prove a risk is not applicable.

## Loss events and effective controls
When a control is described as effective but loss events exist for the same process and risk, ask for the root cause analysis and for control testing evidence. A loss event does not automatically prove a control failure: the event may predate the control, may have a different cause, or the control may be only partly effective. The finding should go to a human reviewer as a question, not as a conclusion.

## Missing KRI coverage (information only)
The absence of a Key Risk Indicator does not say anything about whether a control is effective. The owner may not have needed a KRI for this risk. A KRI that breaches its threshold is the signal that a control may be ineffective; a missing KRI is not. Where loss events exist and no KRI is assigned, Second Line may ask whether a KRI would add value, as an optional monitoring observation and not as a control finding.

## Risk missing from the previous RCSA
If an applicable risk was not part of the previous RCSA, ask what changed in the process, systems or environment, and whether the earlier assessment was incomplete. Treat the gap as a completeness question for the reviewer.

## Applicable risk without a control
An applicable risk with no described control means the residual exposure equals the inherent exposure until a control is defined. Ask the owner to define and assign a control or to document why the exposure is accepted, and to route acceptance through the appropriate governance.

## Control without supporting evidence
A control that is described but has no testing record or evidence is an anomaly. Ask for the latest test result, the control owner and the date of the last review before relying on the control description.

## Process execution error
Process execution errors arise from manual steps, unclear procedures or weak handovers. Useful challenge points: volume of manual steps, four-eyes review, reconciliation breaks, and whether recent errors were caused by a new product or staffing change.

## Third-party failure
Third-party failure covers outages, service level breaches and insolvency of vendors. Useful challenge points: critical vendors involved in the process, exit plans, service level reporting, concentration on one provider and recent incidents reported publicly.

## System outage
System outages interrupt the process. Useful challenge points: recovery time objectives, failover tests, incident history, change management and dependencies on legacy systems.

## Data quality error
Data quality errors lead to wrong decisions or reports. Useful challenge points: validation at data entry, reconciliation between systems, ownership of critical data and previous data incidents.

## Unauthorized access or fraud
Unauthorized access or fraud covers external attacks and internal misuse. Useful challenge points: access reviews, segregation of duties, monitoring and alerting, recent fraud cases and authentication strength.

## Regulatory non-compliance
Regulatory non-compliance arises when the process does not meet legal or supervisory requirements. Useful challenge points: which rules apply, who monitors regulatory change, past findings from audits or supervisors, and training of staff.

## Key person dependency
Key person dependency means the process relies on one or a few individuals. Useful challenge points: documented procedures, deputies, knowledge transfer, recent absences or departures and concentration of access rights.
